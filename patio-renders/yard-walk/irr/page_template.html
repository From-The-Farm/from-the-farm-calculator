Where to connect to water

The capped riser under the kitchen counter

Your video shows a black riser with capped outlets about 3 ft east of the planned sink, under the back counter of the outdoor kitchen. Right beside it, where the sink goes, is a tall white pipe that is probably the sink drain. The riser is almost certainly the water line roughed in for the kitchen. Tee the sprinklers off it, add a 1″ shut-off valve, and run the mainline east under the pad and the walk to the fence strip.

Before you buy parts, open one cap to confirm it is live house water, and see Check your water first. A second capped stub with a coil of black pipe sits inside the new lawn by the east walk. Find out what it is, then use it or cap it below grade before the sod goes down.

Before the concrete is poured

Two sleeves have to go in before the new pad and walk are poured:

  • A 2″ sleeve for the mainline, from the riser east under the pad and the east walk.
  • A 3″ sleeve for both zone pipes and the valve wire, under the east walk at the lawn’s NE corner.

Bury the mainline 12–18″ deep and the zone pipes 8–12″ deep.

Equipment by the east fence

Backflow preventer (PVB) in the strip between the east walk and the fence, 12″ above the lawn so it can protect the house water. Valve box with three 1″ valves next to it: zone 1, zone 2, and a spare for a future drip zone along the fences. Controller on the pavilion post by the kitchen, which will have power. About 30 ft of valve wire runs through the walk sleeve.

Head schedule

Zones and watering

Zone 1 (north half): 6 heads, {{Z1GPM}} GPM. Zone 2 (south half and centre): 6 heads, {{Z2GPM}} GPM. The two zones run one after the other, never together.

Starting schedule for peak summer: 3 days a week, two 35-minute cycles per zone with an hour between them, about 1.4″ a week. Use about half that in spring and fall. MP rotators put water down slowly (0.4″ an hour), so long run times are normal and the water soaks in without running off. A weather-based controller adjusts this for you. Stay within your city’s watering days.

Check your water first

  1. Pressure. Screw a $10 hose-bib pressure gauge onto an outside faucet with all water off. You want 55–80 psi.
  2. Flow. Open that faucet all the way and time how long a 5-gallon bucket takes to fill. 30 seconds is 10 GPM. You want at least 9 GPM.
  3. Lower than that? Split the lawn into three zones of about 5 GPM each. The same heads and pipes work; only the valve wiring changes.

If your lot has pressurized secondary irrigation water, use that instead of house water. It usually has more flow and needs no PVB.

Install order

  1. Before the concrete work, confirm the riser is live water, lay both sleeves, and run the 1″ mainline to the fence strip.
  2. After the concrete cures, set the PVB, the valve box and the valves in the strip. Pressure-test the mainline.
  3. Grade the lawn area. Paint the head positions and pipe runs from this drawing, then trench.
  4. Lay the zone pipes and glue the fittings. Flush each zone with the heads off.
  5. Install the bodies on swing pipe, flush with grade. Add the nozzles, then set each arc and radius from the schedule.
  6. Run each zone and walk the lawn. Trim radii where water lands on the concrete.
  7. Wire the valves to the controller, set the schedule, and lay the sod.

How the layout was chosen. Heads go at every corner and along each edge so each head reaches its neighbours (head-to-head spacing). One full-circle head covers the middle. Head positions, arcs and radii were then tuned in a simulation of the whole lawn. Lower-quarter uniformity came out at {{DU}}, with about {{OVER}}% of the water landing past the lawn edge. A real system typically lands around 0.7, which is still good for lawn. All MP rotators put down water at the same rate whatever their arc, so the zones stay even.
