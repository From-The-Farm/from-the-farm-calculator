Building a Robotics Engineer

A multi-year plan for a ten-year-old who's interested in robotics — built around free resources, a $0 first month, and the four disciplines that actually make an engineer. Spend nothing until the diagnostic month is done.

00

The honest framing

Four pillars
+ one meta-skill

Robotics engineering isn't one skill. It's four disciplines stacked on each other, plus one meta-skill that matters more than any of them.

01

Mechanical

How things move, hold together, transmit force. Gears, linkages, chassis, CAD.

02

Electronics

Power, signals, sensors, actuators. Circuits, motors, voltage, breadboards.

03

Software

The sense → decide → act loop. Scratch, then blocks, then Python.

04

Math & physics

The language the other three run on. Ratios now, trigonometry later.

→

Debugging

The actual job. Robots fail constantly. Isolate a variable. Test one thing.

At ten, the goal is not to "learn robotics." The goal is to keep the fire lit — a kid still interested at thirteen beats a kid who was skilled at eleven and burned out — to go broad and shallow across all four pillars so he finds which one he loves, and to build the habit of finishing and documenting projects.

The guiding principle: a finished ugly robot beats an unfinished beautiful one. Ship small things often.

The single biggest predictor of whether he's still doing this at sixteen isn't talent. It's whether the early experience felt like his project or your project.

01

Do this first: the 30-day kickstart

Cost
$0

Spend nothing for the first month. This isn't frugality — it's diagnosis. You're finding out what kind of interested he is before you spend money. Some kids are builders who tolerate code; some are coders who tolerate building. The kit you buy should match the kid you actually have.

Everything below is free, browser-based, and needs no hardware.

Week 1Free

Scratch

Does he like making things behave?

  • Account at scratch.mit.edu (parent-created under 13)
  • Three tutorials, then one original game
  • Stretch: Harvard's free CS50 Scratch
Week 2Free

micro:bit simulator

Does he like sensors?

  • makecode.microbit.org — has a built-in simulator, no hardware needed
  • Reaction timer, step counter, compass
Week 3Free

VEXcode VR

Does he like robots specifically?

  • A free virtual robot in the browser, no download
  • Drive it, then program it to run a maze
  • Loops and sensor conditions
Week 4Free

Tinkercad

Does he like designing physical things?

  • 3D Design, Circuits and Codeblocks in one free tool
  • Circuits is a real simulator — burn out a virtual LED for free

End of month: the diagnostic conversation

Ask him — and watch, don't just listen:

  • Which week did he lose track of time?
  • Which week did he ask to keep going past the timer?
  • Which week did he show someone else what he made, unprompted?

That's where your money goes. If the answer is "none of them," that's real information too — robotics may be a passing interest, and at ten that is completely fine.

02

The roadmap

Four phases
Age 10 → 14

Move on when the milestone is done, not when the calendar says so. Some kids take two years on Phase 1. Some blow through it in three months.

Phase 1 · Months 1–6Age 10

Blocks and first hardware

Goal: the sense → decide → act loop becomes intuitive, and he owns a robot that reacts to the physical world.

Software
Continue Scratch; add Code.org CS Fundamentals, then CS Discoveries. Move from Scratch to MakeCode blocks on real hardware.
Hardware
micro:bit v2 + SparkFun Inventor's Kit (~$50–60). Cheapest path to real sensors and motors, enormous free curriculum, and it runs blocks now and MicroPython later — so the board doesn't go obsolete when his skills grow.
Mechanical
Cardboard, hot glue, LEGO from the toy bin. Chassis design is a design skill, not a buying decision. 4-H "Junk Drawer Robotics" builds robots from household items with no computer at all.
Math
Fractions, ratios and proportions, decimals, negatives, the coordinate plane, angles and area. Name the connection out loud: a gear ratio is a fraction; field position is a coordinate pair.
Phase 2 · Months 6–12Age 10–11

Text code and a team

Goal: he writes real code in a text editor, and he builds alongside other kids.

Software
MakeCode blocks → MicroPython on the same micro:bit. Same board, same projects, new language — the least painful blocks-to-text jump available. Pair it with Al Sweigart's Invent Your Own Computer Games with Python, free online and explicitly written to be understood by ages 10–12.
Team
Join one. See section 04 — this is the highest-value item in the whole plan.
CAD
Tinkercad → design a custom bracket or wheel, then print it. Use your library's makerspace rather than buying a printer.
Math
Pre-algebra: variables, expressions, solving for x, unit conversion.
Phase 3 · Year 2Age 11–12

Systems and intelligence

Goal: he moves from "a board that runs my code" to "a computer that perceives."

Hardware
Raspberry Pi or ESP32 — networking, a camera, real compute.
Software
Python properly: functions, files, libraries. Introduce Git. Real engineers version their work; starting at eleven is a superpower.
Electronics
Breadboard → soldering (see section 08). His first solder project should have 5–15 joints and visibly do something — light up, buzz, or move.
AI
Teachable Machine — train an image, sound or pose model in an afternoon, no code. Then Machine Learning for Kids to wire a trained model into Scratch or Python.
Math
Algebra 1 track.
Phase 4 · Year 3Age 12–13

Depth and identity

Goal: he stops being "a kid who does robotics" and becomes "a kid who is building this specific thing."

Specialize
By now he'll lean mechanical, electrical, software or AI. Let him. Depth in one pillar plus literacy in the others is exactly how real engineers are shaped.
CAD
Graduate Tinkercad → Onshape: professional parametric CAD, free education plan.
Simulation
Webots (free, open-source, used in industry and research) and robotbenchmark.net — program simulated robots in Python in the browser. This is what university robotics actually looks like.
Math
Algebra 2, geometry, intro trigonometry. Trig is the gate — arms, turning geometry and kinematics are all trig, and this is where kids get filtered out of robotics.
Publish
Coolest Projects — a free, worldwide, non-competitive showcase. An ideal first public demo.

The weekly rhythm

~4–5 hrs
per week

What works is short and frequent, plus one long block. Build sessions need setup and cleanup time; code sessions don't.

WhenLengthWhat
2 weeknights45 minCode, tutorials, simulator work
1 weekend block2 hoursPhysical building — needs the table and the mess
Every day15 minKhan Academy math
Monthly30 minDemo night — he shows the family what he built

Resist going higher at this age. Over-scheduling is the number one way this ends.

Demo night is not optional. A recurring, low-stakes deadline is what converts "things I'm tinkering with" into "things I finished." It's also where he practises explaining technical work to non-technical people — which is, unglamorously, most of an engineer's actual job.

How to find something local

1. Call your county 4-H extension office — cheapest path to a real club, and they'll know what else exists locally.
2. Search firstinspires.org and robotevents.com for nearby teams.
3. Ask his school — many have teams that don't advertise.
4. Check the public library; many run makerspaces and clubs.
5. NASA's Robotics Alliance Project keeps a competition directory.

If there's no team near you, start one

Both FLL and VEX support new teams. You need two to ten kids and one adult coach.

You do not need to know robotics to coach. You need to book a room, keep a schedule, and ask good questions — the kids figure out the robot. Recruit two other families and you have a team.

The budget ladder

Spend on
evidence

The most common and most expensive mistake is buying Tier 3 first. A $400 kit does not create interest. It creates guilt — his, for not using it, and yours, for having bought it.

Each tier has a gate. Don't move up until the gate is met.

TierCostWhatGate to unlock
0$0The 30-day kickstart. Scratch, MakeCode, VEXcode VR, TinkercadStart here. No exceptions.
1~$50–60micro:bit v2 + SparkFun Inventor's KitHe finished the 30 days and asked for more
2~$100–160Motors & chassis (micro:bot kit), or Elegoo: UNO Basic Starter (~$20) / ESP-32 Super Starter (~$36) / Smart Robot Car V4 (~$76)He completed 3+ hardware projects without being prompted
3~$300–500Team registration + competition kit (VEX IQ or SPIKE Prime), or a 3D printerSustained 6+ months, and he wants to compete
4Your timeDriving to meetings, sitting through tournaments, learning alongside himThis is the real investment — and the one that actually matters

Ways to spend less

  • Library makerspaces — 3D printing and laser cutting, often free. Use these instead of buying a printer for at least two years
  • School STEM lab — ask what's sitting unused
  • Borrow from a local team — teams often have spare kits between seasons
  • Buy used — VEX IQ and LEGO kits hold up well and turn over secondhand when teams graduate
  • Financial aid — most competitions offer it and most teams fundraise. Ask; it's normal and expected
06

The math track — the part everyone skips

15 min
per day

Kids don't quit robotics because they stop liking robots. They quit because the math got ahead of them. Robotics engineering is applied mathematics with a battery attached.

Fifteen minutes a day on Khan Academy is enough. The dose matters less than the consistency.

AgeTopicsWhy it's robot math
10–11Fractions, ratios, proportions, decimals, negatives, coordinate plane, angles, areaGear ratios are fractions. Field position is coordinates. Turning is angles
11–12Pre-algebra: variables, expressions, solving equations, unit conversionA variable in code and a variable in algebra are the same idea
12–14Algebra 1, geometry, intro trigonometryTrig is the gate. Arms, turning geometry, kinematics
14+Algebra 2, precalculus, calculus, linear algebra, physicsControl theory, computer vision, dynamics

The technique that makes this work

Every time he hits a robot problem, name the math out loud.

"Your robot overshoots the line by about 20% every time. That's a ratio problem — what if we scaled the motor power by the same fraction?"

Do that a hundred times and math stops being a school subject and becomes a tool he reaches for.

07

The parent playbook

Your job

The highest-leverage thing you can do: don't fix his bugs

When he's stuck, you will know the answer before he does. Do not say it. Ask these five questions instead — this is the debugging ritual, and it is the entire career in five lines.

  1. What did you expect to happen?
  2. What actually happened?
  3. What's different between those two?
  4. What's the smallest test that tells you which part is wrong?
  5. Change one thing. Test it. Repeat.

A ten-year-old who internalises this is doing what professional engineers do all day. A ten-year-old whose dad fixes the robot has a working robot and no skill.

Rules of thumb

  • Let him choose the projects. Interest beats curriculum, every time
  • Never say "that's too hard," and never say "I was bad at math." Kids inherit that instantly and permanently
  • Ship small, ship often. A five-line program that makes a sprite dance today beats an ambitious robot still half-built in March
  • Document everything. Photos, a build journal, short videos. A three-year portfolio at thirteen is genuinely extraordinary — for programs, for scholarships, and for his own sense of momentum
  • Learn alongside him. You need to know none of this. "I don't know, let's find out" is the correct and best answer
  • Take breaks. A walk around the block resets a frustrated kid faster than another twenty minutes of trying
  • Celebrate the failures that taught something. The robot that caught fire is a better story and a better lesson than the one that worked first try

Know the difference between two kinds of frustrated

Frustrated and leaning in

Jaw set, still trying things.

Sit with him. Don't touch anything. This is learning happening in real time.

Frustrated and shutting down

Pushing the robot away. "This is stupid."

Stop. Snack, walk, come back tomorrow. Pushing through this one costs you months.

Warning signs, and how to adapt

What you seeWhat to do
Only wants to build, never codeFine. Lean mechanical. Code will arrive when a build needs it
Only wants to code, never buildFine. Use simulators — VEXcode VR, Webots. Hardware can come later
Interest drops for 3+ weeksBack off completely. Don't push, don't guilt. Interest usually returns if it isn't forced — pressure is what kills it permanently
Races ahead of the planLet him. Skip phases. Get him onto a team with older kids as fast as possible
Wants to quit entirelyLet him, cleanly and without disappointment. He's ten. The skills transfer to whatever's next
08

Safety

Non-
negotiable

Soldering

Appropriate from around age 10 with close supervision. Readiness is about fine motor control and maturity with hot tools, not birthdays.

Lead-free solder · ventilated area or fume fan · safety glasses always · the iron always returns to the stand, enforced absolutely from the first minute. First project: 5–15 through-hole joints, and it must visibly work when finished.

Electrical

Battery and USB voltages only. No mains wiring, ever. Never leave LiPo batteries charging unattended.

Tools & printers

Safety glasses for any cutting or drilling; an adult operates anything that spins fast. 3D printer hot ends and heated beds cause real burns — supervise and ventilate.

Online accounts (COPPA)

Under-13 accounts on Scratch and Tinkercad need parent setup. Onshape requires 13+, or a school or qualified adult authorising the account. Check each platform's under-13 policy as you go.
